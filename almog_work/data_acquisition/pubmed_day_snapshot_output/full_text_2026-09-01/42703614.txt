IntroductionTick-borne encephalitis (TBE) is defined as an infection with the tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) that results in signs and symptoms of central nervous system inflammation (e.g. meningitis, encephalitis, encephalomyelitis) [1]. TBEV infections are the most common arbovirus infections in Europe. TBEV (Orthoflavivirus encephalitidis) is a flavivirus with five recognized subtypes [1,2]. Although the European, Siberian, and the Far Eastern TBEV subtypes have been identified in Europe, almost all TBEV infections in Europe are caused by the European subtype [1,2]. TBEV is primarily transmitted to humans by the bite of an Ixodes tick [3]. Humans may also become infected through the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products derived from a TBEV-infected animal [4]. The sheep tick, I. ricinus, which is commonly found in forested and humid areas in Europe, is the predominant vector for the European subtype and the geographic distribution of surveillance-reported TBE cases in Europe reflects the distribution of I ricinus in Europe [3,5].After the incubation period, an infection with TBEV may result in a diphasic illness with an initial phase characterized by a nonspecific febrile illness, an interphase with temporary improvement, and a second phase with the onset of neurological signs and symptoms. Some TBEV-infected individuals only experience an initial febrile illness, and some patients with TBE do not recall having had a preceding febrile illness [6]. Although some individuals infected with TBEV do not seek medical care, either because they have an asymptomatic infection or because they experience mild symptoms, many TBEV-infected individuals seek medical care (i.e. have a medically attended TBEV infection) [1,2]. There is no specific treatment for TBEV infection, and it canbe life-threatening [7]. The case fatality rate of hospitalized TBE cases in Europe is 1% to 2% [1,2]. Furthermore, TBEV infection in patients with TBE may also result in long-term neurological sequelae which may include cognitive dysfunction and significant impairment of quality of life [1,2].TBE is an increasing public health threat in Europe. In recent years, many European countries have experienced a geographic expansion of TBEV endemic areas and an increase in the incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases [8]. Currently, at least 25 European countries have one or more TBEV endemic areas [9]. Almost all European countries conduct public health surveillance for TBE cases but surveillance approaches and case definitions vary; some countries report only TBE cases (i.e. TBEV infections with central nervous system involvement) and some countries report all TBEV-infected cases [10]. The countries with the highest reported TBE incidence include the Baltic countries (Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania), the Czech Republic, Slovenia, and Switzerland [10,11]. The number of TBE cases reported to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) has been increasing. In the ECDC’s most recent surveillance summary, 20 countries reported 3516 cases (0.8 cases per 100,000 population) to the ECDC in 2022 [11]. TBE vaccines, which have been demonstrated to be highly effective, provide the most effective protection against TBE [12]. At least 32 European countries recommend TBE vaccination for residents of, and travelers to, TBEV endemic areas [13]. However, TBE vaccine uptake is suboptimal in many countries [14].Seroprevalence studies are commonly used to identify the proportion of the general population that is currently, or was previously, infected with an infectious agent. Furthermore, for diseases with established public health surveillance systems, the estimated incidence of current or previous infections derived from a seroprevalence study can be compared with the incidence of surveillance-reported cases to evaluate the degree of ascertainment of infections by surveillance and thereby elucidate the full disease burden pyramid (Figure 1). For example, a “seroprevalence-based approach” to evaluate the extent of under-ascertainment by public health surveillance systems has been used for Lyme borreliosis in many European countries [15]. Until recently, such an approach was not possible for TBE, particularly in countries with TBE vaccine use, because seroprevalence studies could not determine whether elevated anti-TBEV immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies were due to TBE vaccination or TBEV infection [16].Figure 1Burden of disease pyramid for tick-borne encephalitis (TBE), and the seroprevalence-based approach to estimate the under-ascertainment of medically attended TBE virus (TBEV) infections by TBE surveillance.Figure 1 dummy alt textAbbreviations: TBE, tick-borne encephalitis; TBEV, tick-borne encephalitis virus.The availability in 2018 of a new testing approach for detecting anti-TBEV IgG nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) antibodies, which are elevated in TBEV-infected individuals but not in individuals vaccinated with a European TBE vaccine provides an opportunity to conduct TBE seroprevalence studies in Europe using NS1 IgG antibodies to determine the prevalence of TBEV infections even in regions where TBE vaccines are used [16]. A recent application of NS1 IgG antibody testing in Germany described the “manifestation index” for TBE, which is the proportion of TBEV-infected individuals (identified by having anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies) who are diagnosed with TBE at the hospital [17]. Therefore, to better understand the TBE burden in Europe, we conducted a seroprevalence-based approach to estimate under-ascertainment of medically attended TBEV infections by TBE surveillance.MethodsTwo literature searches were performed to identify seroprevalence studies that reported the prevalence of anti-TBEV NS1 IgG antibodies in a study area within a European country that conducts TBE surveillance. Articles identified in the searches were excluded if the seroprevalence study was conducted among high-risk groups (i.e. individuals such as foresters who are at high risk of becoming TBEV infected), or the study included < 300 participants. The first literature search, the results of which were summarized in an article published in 2024 [18], was a systematic literature search to identify articles published from 1959-2024; however, since the NS1 IgG assays became available in 2018 [16], we excluded articles found in the first search that were published before 2018. The second literature search, which was conducted to identify articles published after the first search, was of articles published in 2025 to March 31, 2026. Details of the first search, including the PRISMA diagram, have been published [18]. Both searches were conducted in PubMed, EmBase, and CABI Direct (Global Health), had no language restrictions, and used the search terms “TBE”, “tick-borne encephalitis”, and “surveillance”.The number of individuals with an incident TBEV infection in a year in the study area of the seroprevalence study was estimated from the number of individuals with anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies in the seroprevalence study using the formula: I=P/D, where I is the number of individuals with an incident TBEV infection in the study area in one year, P is the number of individuals in the study area with anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies, and D is the duration, in years, of detection of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies in a TBEV-infected individual. An estimate of the duration of detection of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies was derived from a PubMed search of articles published in 2018-2025. An estimate of the proportion of individuals with an incident TBEV infection who sought medical care was also derived from a PubMed search of articles published from 2010-2025. This proportion was then multiplied by the number of individuals with an incident TBEV infection to derive the number of individuals with an incident medically attended TBEV infection. The number of surveillance-reported TBE cases corresponding to the area and year of the seroprevalence studies were derived from public health surveillance reports available on the websites of the appropriate public health institutes. Population estimates were abstracted from Eurostat (https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat).For each seroprevalence study, we then derived the following 5 estimates: (1) incidence of TBEV infections in the study area which is the number of individuals with an incident TBEV infection per 100,000 population per year of the study; (2) incidence of medically attended TBEV infections which is the incidence of TBEV infections in the study area times the proportion of individuals with an incident TBEV infection who sought medical care; (3) incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases which is the number of surveillance-reported TBE cases in the area and year of the seroprevalence study divided by the population in that area and year; (4) manifestation index which is the incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases divided by incidence of TBEV infections; and (5) under-ascertainment multiplier which is the incidence of medically attended TBEV infections divided by the incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases and, therefore, represents the number of medically attended TBEV infections that are estimated to occur for each surveillance-reported TBE case. All incidence estimates were expressed per 100,000 population per year (PPY).ResultsOf the 47 anti-TBEV IgG antibody seroprevalence studies identified by the first literature review published by Kelly et al. [18], 34 (72%) were excluded because they were carried out among high-risk groups, they were done in a European country which did not conduct TBE surveillance, or they included < 300 participants (Figure 2). After the remaining 13 articles underwent full-text review, nine studies were excluded because they did not include individuals with anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies or they had less information than a more recent nationwide seroprevalence study. The four studies from the first literature search were combined with five studies identified in the second literature search which resulted in nine anti-TBEV IgG NS1 seroprevalence studies conducted of the general population in a country or area of a country with TBE surveillance [17,[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. The under-ascertainment multiplier ranged from 0.9 (95% CI 0.6-1.4) in the Åland Islands, Finland, to 6.0 (95% CI 5.0-7.1) in Baden-Wuerttemberg, Germany (Table 2).DiscussionUsing conservative estimates for the duration of NS1 IgG antibodies and the proportion of TBEV-infected individuals who seek medical care, we derived estimates of the under-ascertainment of symptomatic TBEV infections by TBE surveillance. Our estimates, derived from data from published anti-TBEV IgG NS1 seroprevalence studies, indicate that in eight of the nine areas where the seroprevalence studies were conducted, there were more individuals with a TBEV infection who sought medical care than were reported in TBE surveillance; the exception was in the Åland Islands where it appeared that all medically attended TBEV infections are ascertained in surveillance. Although it is likely that medically attended TBEV infections that are not reported to surveillance are milder than surveillance-reported TBE cases, the under-ascertainment multipliers indicate that in the area where, and year when, the seroprevalence studies were conducted, the incidence of medically attended TBEV infection was several-fold higher than surveillance-reported TBE incidence. Since disease incidence is a key measure for estimating disease burden, our results add to the growing evidence of the substantial TBE burden in Europe.TBE surveillance data are available in many European countries; however, based upon our literature searches, appropriate anti-TBEV IgG NS1 seroprevalence studies were only available for seven countries. In our analysis, we did not use seroprevalence studies of individuals at increased risk for tick exposure or increased risk of TBE (e.g. studies of forest workers) because such studies would yield a higher prevalence of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies and are not appropriate for estimating the under-ascertainment of medically attended TBEV infections by surveillance, which reflects the general population.The under-ascertainment of medically attended TBEV-infected cases by surveillance varied. Using a nationwide seroprevalence study conducted in Denmark, a country where TBEV has only recently emerged and where there are only a limited number of TBEV endemic areas [8] and a low TBE incidence [11], there was a very low (0.1%) prevalence of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies and a low incidence (0.5/100,000 PPY) of medically attended TBEV infections. Given the recent emergence of TBEV infection in Denmark, it is perhaps not surprising that there appears to be low awareness of TBEV by clinicians and the public in Denmark. However, we estimated that there are five medically attended TBEV infections for each surveillance-reported TBE case in Denmark. In contrast, in the Åland Islands in Finland, a TBEV endemic area with a high TBE incidence and high TBE vaccine uptake [27], there was a high (7.0%) prevalence of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies and we estimate that all medically attended TBEV-infected cases are ascertained by surveillance even though the surveillance case definition in the Åland Islands only includes TBE cases [10]. The low under-ascertainment multiplier in the Åland Islands likely reflects the high awareness of TBE by the public and clinicians in the Åland Islands [27].All regions in Latvia are considered TBEV endemic and there is a high incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases in the country [28]. However, TBE vaccine uptake in Latvia is only moderate [14]. In our study, we used a nationwide seroprevalence study in Latvia to estimate there are approximately two TBEV-infected individuals for each surveillance-reported case in the country despite having a surveillance case definition that includes all TBEV-infected individuals (not just TBE cases) [10]. In Switzerland, which also has a surveillance case definition that includes all TBEV-infected cases [10] and which has many TBEV endemic areas and high TBE incidence, we also estimate that there are approximately two TBEV-infected individuals for each surveillance-reported case. The surveillance case definition in Germany also includes all TBEV-infected cases [10], and the two Germany seroprevalence studies were conducted in TBEV endemic areas with moderate TBE incidence and moderate vaccine uptake [14]. The estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers in Germany indicate that for every surveillance-reported case, there are four to six medically attended TBEV infections. It is unlikely that this under-ascertainment in Germany is because of under-reporting of laboratory-confirmed TBEV infections, as all laboratory-confirmed cases are statutorily notifiable; it is more likely that the under-ascertainment in Germany is due to under-testing [29]. The two Austrian seroprevalence studies were also conducted in TBEV endemic areas with moderate TBE incidence but Austria has a high TBE vaccine uptake [14]. Similar to Germany, the estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers in Austria indicate that for every surveillance-reported case, there are approximately four to five medically attended TBEV infections. Finally, in Sweden, a country with TBEV endemic areas particularly along the coast and the large lakes, and in the southern part of the country [30] and with a moderate TBE incidence and TBE vaccine uptake [14], the estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers indicate that for every surveillance-reported case, there are approximately three medically attended TBEV infections.There are limitations of our study to bear in mind. We sought to include seroprevalence studies that reflect the prevalence of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies in the general population. However, the samples used in the seroprevalence studies, with the exception of the samples from Switzerland and Latvia, were from blood donors who are likely healthier than the general population and more likely to conduct outdoor activities, which may expose them to TBEV. In contrast, however, blood donors are more likely to be younger and may be more likely to be vaccinated, which would reduce their likelihood of becoming TBEV infected. Despite these possible biases, it is judged that the samples used in these studies largely represented the general adult population. An important factor contributing to the variability in our estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers is the imprecision of the seroprevalence estimates of some of the seroprevalence studies due to small sample sizes; in an attempt to mitigate this concern, we only included studies with ≥300 participants. The study in the Åland Islands only had 300 participants but the adult population on the Åland Islands is <30,000; the other seroprevalence studies had 1000 to 4600 samples. Another limitation is that it was not possible to derive age-specific under-ascertainment multipliers. Furthermore, since the participants in most of the seroprevalence studies included only adults (but TBE surveillance ascertains cases from all ages), we had to extrapolate the results of the seroprevalence studies to all ages.Another potential bias of our study is that, due to the limited published data, there is uncertainty in the two key assumptions used to derive the estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers (i.e. the duration of detection of anti-TBEV IgG NS1 antibodies in a TBEV-infected individual, and the proportion of TBEV-infected individuals who seek medical care). For these assumptions, we used estimates (20-year duration of detection and 10% care seeking proportion) that can be considered conservative as use of a shorter duration of detection or a higher care seeking proportion would result in higher under-ascertainment estimates. The assumption of a 20-year duration of detection was based on results described by Girl et al. [16] based upon the testing of collected sera from individual patients with laboratory-confirmed TBE diagnosed 5, 10, 23, and 28 years previously. The assumption that 10% of TBEV-infected individuals seek medical care was based upon published reports that describe that many TBEV-infected individuals are not diagnosed with TBE (i.e. have a “subclinical” infection). Further studies are needed to fill these data gaps on the duration of detection of NS1 IgG antibodies and the proportion of TBEV-infected individuals who seek medical care. Importantly, the relative consistency in the observed under-ascertainment multipliers estimated from the nine studies supports the appropriateness of the estimates we used for these two key assumptions. Nevertheless, we acknowledge, given the limited data to support these key assumptions, that the under-ascertainment multiplier estimates reported in our study should be viewed as first approximations and that further work is needed to assess these assumptions and the estimates of under-ascertainment.Another limitation is that, with the exception of Denmark and Latvia which utilized nationwide seroprevalence studies, our estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers are only for the specific region where the seroprevalence study was conducted. This is particularly important for the study in the Åland Islands where TBEV is highly endemic. Extrapolating the results from a specific region to other parts of the country or to other countries may not be appropriate. Additionally, caution should be used when comparing the under-ascertainment multipliers between countries. Countries have different approaches for TBE surveillance, including different case definitions and surveillance reporting systems.This study represents the first application of a seroprevalence-based approach for deriving estimates of the under-ascertainment of medically attended TBEV infections by TBE surveillance. The aim of this study was to improve the understanding of the incidence of medically attended TBEV infections and the TBE burden in Europe. Since surveillance systems are not intended to ascertain all cases of medically attended TBEV infections, it is expected that the estimated incidence of medically attended TBEV infections is higher than the incidence of surveillance-reported TBE cases. Quantifying the extent of the under-ascertainment of medically attended TBEV infections by TBE surveillance is a useful tool for understanding the TBE burden.ConclusionsDespite assumptions necessitated by data gaps, our estimates of the under-ascertainment multipliers derived from the data from published NS1 seroprevalence studies indicate that there are many more medically attended TBEV infections than are ascertained by public health surveillance for TBE. Further studies are needed to fill these data gaps, particularly with respect to the duration of NS1 IgG antibodies and the proportion of TBEV-infected individuals who seek medical care. Using conservative assumptions for these data gaps, our estimates of under-ascertainment suggest that the disease burden of TBEV is greater than estimated solely on surveillance data. These findings support the need for increased TBEV awareness and for more frequent diagnostic testing for TBEV infection especially in TBEV endemic areas. Furthermore, given the increasing public health threat posed by TBE in Europe, increased efforts are needed to increase TBE vaccination in persons at high risk of TBE.CRediT authorship contribution statementFrederick J.

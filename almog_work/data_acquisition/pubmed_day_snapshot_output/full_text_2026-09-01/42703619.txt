Interest in infectious diseases (ID) typically develops during or before medical school and is influenced by strong microbiology training, mentorship, and early clinical exposure [1-3]. Scholarly mentorship may facilitate meaningful faculty-student connections and provide early exposure to the specialty [1-3]. Case reports and poster presentations may promote academic writing and presentation skills for students with little prior scholarly activity experience [4-6] and may help spark interest in the clinical subject. There may be limited opportunities to practice and develop these skills in a low-stress environment, as the case report acceptance rates for most mainstream general medical journals are lower, with variable acceptance rates for abstracts at national meetings [7]. State medical societies may help to mitigate this through local/regional abstract competitions, and student ID Interest groups (IDIG) can serve as a vehicle to bring students and faculty together. We describe the development of a student-initiated, case-based, mentored scholarly initiative (IDIG Power Hour) led by an IDIG to foster early medical student academic writing experiences and increase student engagement with ID faculty outside of clinical rotations.METHODSSettingThe University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC) IDIG was established in 2015 in Omaha, Nebraska. The student group supports an average of 40 active members annually and is open to all interested medical students with 2 faculty advisors (adult and pediatric ID). The IDIG hosts 3–4 events per semester to facilitate students’ exposure to the ID specialty. The Nebraska Infectious Diseases Society (NIDS) holds an Annual Meeting with a trainee abstract competition. The first NIDS abstract competition was held in 2022 and was limited to ID fellows and residents interested in ID. In 2023, the event expanded to all Nebraska health professions trainees, including students.IDIG Power Hour DescriptionIDIG student leadership launched an annual 1-hour case-writing pilot (“Power Hour”) in Spring 2023 aimed at increasing mentored student scholarly activity through abstract submissions to the NIDS Annual Meeting. The inaugural IDIG Power Hour paired participating UNMC medical students and ID faculty on available cases, with the intent to write abstracts for presentation. Student leaders requested interesting case submissions from UNMC adult and pediatric ID faculty via email, with 2–3 reminders and nudges from faculty advisors. IDIG leadership recruited Power Hour student participants using the interest group listserv and college-wide emails and then assigned medical student pairs to cases and faculty mentors based on case availability. During the noon Power Hour activity, faculty assessed student mentees’ prior scholarly experiences, discussed case details, helped students identify the teaching points and structure of a clinical presentation abstract, and set timelines for completion of tasks. Limited IDIG funds covered the cost of lunch provided to participants. After the Power Hour, students and faculty collaborated asynchronously on writing and submitting abstracts to the NIDS abstract competition. Some student-faculty teams chose to pursue additional presentation elsewhere, case report publication, and/or further mentorship, with an emphasis on this being a student-driven process.IDIG Power Hour Pilot EvaluationTo evaluate the first 2 years of student experiences in the Power Hour pilot, we distributed a 21-question electronic pre-post survey evaluation via email (Microsoft Forms) to students who participated in the 2023 and 2024 IDIG Power Hour (Supplementary material). Evaluations were distributed at 7 months and 14 months after the inaugural 2023 Power Hour. Surveys were open for a 1-month period following initial distribution, with 2 reminders. Survey questions included student year of study, IDIG membership status, participation year, prior research writing experiences, exposure to the field of infectious diseases, and perceptions of faculty support. Counts and percentages summarize descriptive data. Fisher's exact test was used to assess associations between evaluation questions.RESULTSIn 2023 and 2024, 9 participating ID faculty proposed 19 separate cases, with a mean of 2.2 cases per faculty (Table 1). A total of 38 medical students participated in 2023 and 2024, with 4 students participating in both cycles. Nineteen students responded to the evaluation (55.9% response rate). Most responses came from students who participated in only the 2023 cycle (59.1%, 13/22). Most were second-year medical students at the time of the event (47.4%, 9/19), followed by third-year (31.6%, 8/19) and fourth-year (10.5%, 2/19) medical students. One respondent participated in 2023 and 2024. Most students were also members of the IDIG for only 1–2 years at the time of response (42.1%, 8/19). Of the 19 student pairs that participated in the Power Hour over the 2-year cycle, 17 presented at the NIDS conference, and most student pairs felt somewhat/completely prepared for this case presentation (15/19; 78.9%). Most students reported a neutral to positive level of confidence in their ability to write a case report after the experience (15/19; 78.9%), and the same number also reported either having submitted or planning to submit their case report for publication (15/19; 78.9%). There was no statistically significant association between confidence in case writing and case submission (P = .064). All respondents reported that they would participate in a similar event again and would recommend the Power Hour to other students.DISCUSSIONWe describe a low-cost, student-led initiative that successfully connected medical students with ID faculty mentors through case-based scholarship. Over the first 2 years of the program, 38 students were paired with 9 faculty members to collaborate on 19 clinical cases, resulting in 17 case presentations at the state ID society meeting. Most participating students reported positive faculty support and confidence in case writing, and all participants reported willingness to participate in similar activities in the future. These findings suggest that student interest groups can serve as an effective mechanism for connecting learners with faculty mentors and facilitating early scholarly experiences.Students participating in the Power Hour valued the opportunity to engage with faculty mentors while developing scholarly writing and presentation skills. Involvement in scholarly activity positively influences medical student academic skill development, including critical literature appraisal, scientific writing skills, and clinical observation skills [4, 10, 11]. While some may consider case reports to be the lowest form of scholarship [7], they often provide learners with their first engagement with academic publication and mentorship, potentially paving the way for interest in more advanced types of scholarly activity [11-13]. Additionally, relationships developed between faculty and trainees during these formative scholarly experiences may be as important as the skills acquired through writing and presentation.Nearly all student-faculty teams successfully translated participation in the Power Hour into presentation at the NIDS annual meeting. Beyond presentation at the state meeting, several teams presented their work at other conferences, and 2 teams progressed to peer-reviewed publication. These outcomes demonstrate that the Power Hour served not only as an introductory mentorship experience but also as a pathway to tangible student scholarly outcomes. Informal feedback from both students and mentors since the pilot survey has led to the IDIG reevaluating the process to develop and foster more longitudinal mentorship. Although changes in specialty interest were not a primary outcome of this pilot evaluation, exploratory findings suggested that some participants explored more ID-related experiences following participation.There are some limitations with this study. This was a single-institution pilot evaluation involving a small number of students, which limits generalizability. Additionally, this evaluation focused on student perceptions and outcomes. Faculty mentor experiences were not formally assessed and represent a key area for future study. Participation was voluntary, and many students were affiliated with the IDIG prior to participation. As a result, participants may have entered the program with preexisting interest in ID, limiting interpretation of changes in specialty interest. Future evaluations should include additional cohorts, longer-term scholarly outcomes, and assessments of faculty mentor experiences.CONCLUSIONThe IDIG Power Hour is a feasible, low-cost model that facilitates student-faculty collaboration and scholarly development while providing exposure to ID faculty and clinical cases.Supplementary Materialofag541_Supplementary_Data

Western world. Established risk factors include a personal or familial history of other atopic

comorbidities, genetic predisposition, male sex, and hygienic environment. However, these

factors fail to explain most FA cases. The study aimed to discover additional risk factors for FA
by analyzing a large database. Methods: This retrospective study utilized data from Clalit Health
Services, Israel’s largest healthcare provider. Employing explainable artificial intelligence
methods, the investigation sought to identify variables in the first 6 months of life and parental
factors correlated with FA diagnosis, among children born from January 1, 2006, to August 31,
2021. Results: The analysis encompassed 370,298 children, with FA diagnosed in 6,911 infants
(1.87%). Key findings revealed that high socioeconomic score (SES) (relative risk [RR] = 9.92),

strength included parental atopic comorbidities, blood type, and antibiotics and anti-acid drugs
use. Surprisingly, prematurity and birthweight <2,500 g were correlated with a lower risk of FA
development. Conclusions: The correlations found between those variables and FA do not
explain most FA cases. Moreover, the impact of SESs and ethnicity might be explained by
differences in cultural behaviors that influence the development of FA. This could not be
determined as data such as the age of allergenic foods introduction were unavailable in the
database. Further investigation is warranted to establish causal links and the clinical significance
of these suggested risk factors.

Journal Section: Clinical AllergyEditor's choice

Immunoglobulin E-mediated food allergy (FA) is a growing problem in the Western world [1‒3].
The prevalence of FA in Israel is 2.2–2.8% [4, 5]. Major efforts are invested in preventing the
development of FA [6‒12]. It is accepted that patients with one atopic disease have an increased
risk of developing another atopic comorbidity [13]. Other risk factors for developing FA include
genetic predisposition, family history of atopic comorbidities, male sex, and hygienic
environment [2, 13‒16]. However, these factors do not explain the multitude of patients with FA
without these risk factors. On the other hand, early introduction to food allergens has a
protective effect and helps decrease the chance of developing FA [6‒10] but does not explain
infants who were introduced to allergenic foods later than recommended and did not develop
FA. Most studies conducted to find risk factors involved small cohorts [14, 17, 18] or limited
datasets [19]. Focusing on a broader picture, the present study searched for correlations
between FA and nonspecific medical data during fetal life and the first 6 months of life with the
purpose of identifying additional factors that may act as risk factors or protective factors.

Methods
Study Population
Children born from January 1, 2006, to August 31, 2021, who were insured by Clalit heath
service (CHS), the largest health maintenance organization in Israel, insuring 54% of the
population were included. Criteria used for FA diagnosis: diagnosis of FA by an allergist (see
International Classification of Diseases 9th revision (ICD-9) codes used for diagnosis of FA in
online suppl. Material 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000548032);
diagnosis of FA made by a pediatrician, or family doctor and a prescription for adrenaline
autoinjector; specific IgE blood test result of ≥3.5 kU/L, defined as likely risk of symptoms after
exposure [3, 20], to at least one food allergen and prescription of an adrenaline autoinjector. All
infants who did not match any of the above conditions were included in the nonallergic group.

Data Extracted
The study focused on the first 6 months of life because most FAs manifest in the second half of
the first year of life or even later [5]. To avoid label leakage problems associated with the
diagnostic process, we used information from the period before most FA cases manifest. As the
age at which allergenic food is introduced to an infant’s diet is not registered in the medical
record, we relied on the reports that most parents follow the World Health Organization
recommendations and introduce complementary foods after 6 months of age [21‒23]. For each
infant, the following parameters were extracted from the CHS database:
1. Demographics – sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic score (SES), and number of siblings.
2. Parental diagnosis – comorbidities related to infants’ risk for FA, and other common
comorbidities in young, generally healthy adults.
3. Common maternal diagnoses and laboratory tests during the current pregnancy
(calculated 9 months before infant’s birth).
4. Birth information – gestational age (GA), weight, and mode of delivery.
5. Infants’ medical status during the first 6 months of life:
- Diagnoses
- Medications – all medications purchased by caregivers under the infants’
identification number.

Information regarding data retrieved is detailed in online supplementary Material 2. The ICD-9
and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical-5 (ATC-5) codes used are listed in online supplementary
eTables 1–3. Data were extracted from CHS using the Clalit Research data-sharing platform
powered by MDClone (https://www.mdclone.com).
This study was approved by the Meir Medical Center Ethics Committee (0148-22-MMC).
Informed consent was not required as the data were anonymized. This study follows the
STROBE guidelines for observational research.

Models and Statistical Analyses
XGBoost Model
The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a machine learning algorithm based on regression
trees that solve numerous data science problems efficiently and accurately [24]. In the present
study, an XGBoost model was constructed to predict the binary field indicating FA diagnosis
using the variables mentioned in the Section: Data Extracted. Model performance was evaluated
using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curve metric, which
interpolates the sensitivity and specificity of the model.

Feature Importance
Two methods were used to obtain the importance of the features from the model’s results. The
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) score is a technique to evaluate the importance of input
features of a given machine learning model [25]. The details of this method are described in
online supplementary Material 3. The change to the AUC (Δ-AUC) when the feature is replaced
with a random value obtained by permuting the values of the feature between subjects. The
details of this method are described in online supplementary Material 3.

Randomized Features
The study groups’ characteristics were compared using the Chi-squared test for binary and
categorical variables. p values were reported without correction for multiplicities, as study
aimed to explore a broad range of potential factors associated with the development of FA [26].
Additionally, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated for each variable, with an
SMD ≥0.2 considered clinically significant.

Results
During January 1, 2006 to August 8, 2021, 1,359,480 children were born and insured by CHS. Of
which, 370,298 (27.23%) were born in hospitals owned by CHS with data regarding the delivery
and perinatal period during hospitalization (Fig. 1). The differences between the cohorts are
either small (SMD <0.2) or statistically insignificant (online suppl. eTable 4), further analyses
were done only on infants born in a CHS hospital. FA according to the inclusion criteria was
diagnosed in 6,911 children (1.87%). Statistically significant differences were found between the
allergic and the nonallergic infants in all parameters except for mode of delivery, but the SMD
was ≤0.2 for all parameters except for ethnicity, SES, use of antihistamines and systemic
steroids, diagnosis of atopic dermatitis (AD), number of siblings, maternal smoking, and
maternal anemia (Tables 1, 2).

presented in online supplementary eTable 5. Online supplementary Figure 2b shows the

Demographic Variables
Higher SES increased the risk of FA (Fig. 4a). The relative risk (RR) of infants with SES = 5 to
develop FA was 9.921 compared to infants with SES = 1 (683/26,157 [2.6%] vs. 132/50,151
[0.26%], 95% CI: 8.207–12.001, p < 0.001, SMD = 0.57). The mean RR for increasing SES
compared to SES = 1 was 4.33.

In terms of ethnicity, Jewish infants had a higher risk for FA than Arab infants (RR: 3.83, 95% CI:
3.43–4.28, p < 0.001, SMD = 0.391). The prevalence of FA among Jewish infants was 2.13% vs.
0.56% in the Arab population. Male infants were significantly more likely to develop FA than
females (3,908/6,911 [56.5%] vs. 185,921/363,387 [51.2%], respectively; RR: 1.237, 95% CI: 1.18–
1.297, p < 0.001).
Firstborn infants constituted 52.4% of the FA infants vs. 43.4% of the nonallergic infants (RR:
1.43, 95% CI: 1.36–1.5, p < 0.001, SMD = 0.218). Having two or more siblings was associated with
a protective effect against developing FA, comprising 18.78% of the FA infants vs. 27.03% of the
nonallergic infants (RR: 0.696, 95% CI: 0.662–0.731, p < 0.001). Although with a minor influence,
one sibling also had a protective effect against FA (Table 1; Fig. 4b).

Parental Variables
Parental atopic comorbidities were correlated with higher prevalence of FA in their offspring.
The most significant variable of these diagnoses was maternal allergic rhinitis (AR) (1,815/6,911
[26.3%] vs. 65,806/363,387 [18.1%], respectively; RR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.392–1.51, p < 0.001). Other
maternal atopic comorbidities that correlated with the development of FA were asthma and AD
(Table 1; Fig. 2, 3). Among paternal atopic diagnoses, asthma, and AR correlated with the
development of FA (Table 1; Fig. 2, 3). At least one atopic comorbidity of either maternal or
paternal was found in 64.5% of the FA infants and 55.8% of the nonallergic infants (p < 0.0001),
but the SMD was 0.17. Other parental chronic diagnoses or maternal diagnoses during
pregnancy that were found in correlation to the development of FA were smoking of either
parent, maternal anemia, hypothyroidism, or vitamin deficiency (including iron).

Blood Type
Blood type A for both the mother and the infant were more prevalent in allergic infants
compared to nonallergic infants (1,819/4,356 [41.76%] vs. 86,616/225,691 [38.38%], 95% CI:
1.081–1.219, and 315/953 [33.1%] vs. 14,895/52,319 [28.5%], 95% CI: 1.078–1.415, respectively, p
< 0.001). In contrast, blood type O for both was more prevalent in the nonallergic group
(69,594/225,691 [30.83%] vs. 1,185/4,356 [27.2%], 95% CI: 1.002–1.004, and 25,053/52,319
[47.9%] vs. 401/953 [42.1%], 95% CI: 1.002–1.006, respectively, p < 0.001). It is important to
mention that blood typing is only performed for infants with a clinical indication; therefore, few
infants have a blood type recorded.

Infants’ Variables
Several factors related to labor and the neonatal period were correlated with the development
of FA. As shown in Figure 4c, in the transition to term infants there was a significant increase in
the association with FA. The same pattern was found with birthweight (Fig. 4d). In the transition
to 2,500 g (a normal birthweight for term newborns), there was a significant increase in the
prevalence of FA. The birth month also influences the risk of FA. Infants born in July–December
were at higher risk of developing FA compared to infants born in January–June (3,715/6,911
[53.7%] vs. 187,276/363,387 [51.5%], RR: 1.043, 95% CI: 1.02–1.066, p < 0.001). Other acute
delivery situations (codes included are detailed in online suppl. eTable 3) and epidural
anesthesia during labor were also correlated with FA (Table 2; online suppl. eTable 4).

Infants with AD had 5.3-fold chance of developing FA (1,712/21,639 [7.91%] vs. 5,199/348,659
[1.49%], 95% CI: 5.03–5.595, p < 0.001, SMD = 0.559, average AUC 0.0238). Although asthma was

In addition to AD and asthma, several infant diagnoses correlated with FA (Table 2), including
gastrointestinal tract (GIT) illnesses (2,916/6,911 [42.2%] vs. 120,236/363,387 [33.1%],
respectively; RR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.397–1.536, p < 0.001) and infectious diseases, especially
bacterial diagnoses. The other diagnoses correlated with FA are detailed in Table 2 and Figures
2, 3.
The use of antihistamines, systemic corticosteroids, and inhalations with β-agonists or inhaled
corticosteroids were correlated with the development of FA; however, these were considered as
supporting variables. Another two groups of drugs that were prescribed more often during the
first 6 months of life to the allergic infants were antibiotics and anti-acids (Table 2). Among
infants with FA, 313/6,911 (4.5%) were prescribed anti-acid drugs compared to 6,024/363,387
(1.7%) of the nonallergic infants (RR: 2.72, 95% CI: 2.434–3.047, p < 0.001).

Number of Risk Factors
Looking only at the binary risk factors and excluding the supporting risk factors mentioned
above, the allergic group had an average of 7.69 ± 2.85 risk factors compared to 6.67 ± 2.68 in
the nonallergic group (p < 0.001, SMD = 0.37). The incidence of ≤5 risk factors in infants with FA
was 230:1,000 compared to 347:1,000 in the nonallergic group. Among the allergic infants, 925
(13.4%) did not have any of the risk factors we have found, and 1,806 infants (26.1%) had only
one risk factor.

Discussion
Family and personal history of atopic comorbidities are well-known risk factors contributing to
the development of FA, but they do not explain most FA cases. Other risk factors mentioned in
the literature include firstborn child, Cesarean delivery, and vitamin D deficiency. Information
regarding the contribution of these factors and others is scarce. To our knowledge, the current
study is the largest conducted in the field in a general pediatric population.
The goal of this study was to find new variables that may influence the risk of infants developing
FA. We chose to focus on the prenatal period (represented by parental diagnoses) and the first 6
months of life because we wanted to search for events that happened prior to FA development.
We identified 40 variables that might correlate with the development of FA, including the wellestablished risk factors, such as family and personal history of atopic comorbidities and sex, but
also other less familiar factors. One of the disadvantages of using a large database is that every
minor difference between groups can be statistically significant without clinical importance. For
this reason, we also calculated the SMD for each variable. The only clinically significant
parameters that we found were SES, ethnicity, diagnosis of AD, maternal smoking, maternal
anemia, and number of siblings with borderline SMD. Therefore, the clinical meaning of the
other risk factors needs further investigation.

Demographic and Familial Characteristics
SES and Ethnicity
High SES and Jewish origin were two of the major variables we have found that significantly
correlated with greater chance for developing FA. Although the correlation between SES and FA
is familiar [27], the literature is contradictory. Most studies have found that low SES correlates
with a higher incidence of allergic diseases. However, most of them compared different
populations with many other confounders that could contribute to the correlation, such as
ethnicity [28]. On the other hand, Mullins et al. [29] showed the same direction of correlation we
found. They speculated that differences in health-seeking behaviors support this finding.
Another speculation is based on the hygiene theory, which states that the more hygienic we are,
the more allergic we become [30]. Lower SES is usually correlated with a less hygienic
environment and a higher prevalence of smoking [31]. This may also explain why parental and
specifically maternal smoking was found to be protective against the development of FA. A third
explanation could be differences in the age of exposure to allergenic foods between different
SES groups. The Arab population in Israel has a significantly lower SES [32]. Previous studies
have shown that fewer Arab compared to Jewish women tend to exclusively breastfeed,
resulting in a lower incidence of cow’s milk allergy [7, 33]. This is further supported by a survey
conducted by the Israeli Ministry of Health showing that Arab infants are exposed to
complementary foods at an earlier age [32]. The current study also found that the incidence of
FA was significantly lower in the Arab compared to the Jewish population. Therefore, we
conjecture that the lower prevalence of FA in lower SES groups is related to factors
characterizing this group, including environmental hygiene, differences in feeding practices and
breastfeeding, and not only lower SES.

Number of Siblings
Firstborn in our study is correlated with a higher chance of developing FA. This is supported by
the literature [18, 34], and the explanation is that firstborn children are raised in a more
hygienic environment, which might affect the incidence of allergic comorbidities. To complete
the picture, ≥2 siblings have a protective effect against the development of FA.

Family Atopic Comorbidities
Supporting other studies, we found that family atopic comorbidities correlate with FA [2, 15]. As
shown before [15], the most influential variable was AR of the mother. We found that the
mother’s atopic background has more influence than the father’s on the development of FA in
their offspring. We speculate that when the mother has an atopic comorbidity, the fetus grows
in an “allergic environment” with differences in cytokines that cross the placenta [35]. Looking at
the low SMD of these variables separately and even when combining all the atopic
comorbidities of the two parents together makes this correlation questionable. Moreover, even
in the nonatopic infants 55% of them had a family history of at least one atopic comorbidity.
Additional prospective studies are needed to investigate this difference between maternal and
paternal factors.

Other Maternal Variables
We found that blood type A of the mothers or infants correlates with FA. In contrast, blood type
O (either infant or mother) was found to be protective against FA. To our knowledge, this is the
first time this correlation has been reported. Several studies have been conducted on the
relationship between AR, asthma, and AD, but the results are inconclusive [36]. The hypothesis
behind the relationship between FA and blood groups arose in a study conducted in the 1960s
that found interaction between a variety of pollens with blood group antigens [36].

Child Personal Parameters
Personal History of Atopic Comorbidities
It is well established that infants with AD and asthma are at increased risk of developing FA
[2, 18, 37]. The correlation between AD and FA was also clinically significant in our study. The
relatively low correlation between asthma and FA in our cohort might be explained by our focus
on the first 6 months of life. Asthma is rarely diagnosed at this early age, but it correlates with
the use of systemic steroids and inhalations because wheezing episodes at this age are often
treated with these drugs.

Gestational Age and Birthweight
Term infants had an increased risk for developing FA compared to preterm. This result
correlates with birthweight. This observation supports studies that showed that the hazard ratio
for developing FA favors preterm infants, especially those who were born before 32 GA [19, 38].
Other studies did not find GA a relevant factor for FA development [17]. It is unknown whether
differences in gut and immune maturation between preterm and term infants play a role in
these differences. A study on pigs showed that maturation of the gut and immune system is
delayed in preterm pigs, including the transition from T helper cell (Th)2 to Th1 immune
responses. Regarding gut maturation, they found that many functional activities in the preterm
gut differ from term guts, including enzyme activity and permeability [39]. Additionally, preterm
infants are breastfed for a shorter duration compared to term infants [40‒43]. This correlates
with previous studies showing that breastfed infants have an increased risk for developing FA
[5, 44, 45]. In our opinion, the lower incidence of FA in preterm and low birthweight infants is
due to an immature gut and immune system combined with different feeding practices
between term and preterm infants.

Use of Anti-Acids
This correlates with the development of FA. Moreover, the positive correlation between
diagnosis of GIT illnesses and FA support this relation, as the primary GIT diagnosis in young
infants is gastro-esophageal reflux, which is treated with anti-acid drugs. Infants and adults
treated with anti-acid drugs are prone to develop FA [46]. The suggested mechanism includes
changes in protein degradation and consequently exposure to larger peptides and proteins that
cause sensitization rather than tolerance [47].

Infections during the First 6 Months of Life
Bacterial infections and antibiotic treatments were positively correlated with FA. However, the
direction of correlation of general infectious diagnoses was unclear, and the main difference
between the allergic and nonallergic group was caused by the bacterial infections. Previous
studies have established the connection and theory behind the influence of antibiotic treatment
at early ages and FA [48, 49]. Antibiotic treatments affect the GIT flora and reduce the diversity
of gut microbiome, which is important for developing a healthy immune system with Th1
dominance. Moreover, mild viral infections early in life push the immune system toward Th1
responses [50]. Therefore, the logical conclusion based on our results and the literature is that
mild viral infections protect from developing FA, but bacterial infections and antibiotic
treatments increase the risk.

Neonatal Jaundice
We found an unclear mild correlation between neonatal jaundice and FA. Lee-Sarwar et al. [51]
showed that bilirubin metabolites in stool samples of infants ages 3–6 months negatively
correlate with the development of FA. Our findings support this result. However, this needs
further investigation and understanding of the underlying mechanism.
We found a correlation between FA and other suspected risk factors, and the correlation and
direction of these variables needs to be investigated prospectively. It is important to note that
even within this large cohort with a broad perspective on the first 6 months of life, the most
significant risk factors were birthweight, SES, ethnicity, and AD. Moreover, when excluding these
risk factors, 23% of the infants had fewer than 5 risk factors and none were dominant. As
described, three out of the four risk factors mentioned above are influenced by environmental
differences and feeding practices (birthweight, SES, and ethnicity). We assume that all risk
factors described in this study have some effect on the development of FA, but the major
players are environmental and allergen exposures. This strengthens the assumption that
development of FA is not predestined, and we can influence its development through acquired
factors such as the age of introduction of allergenic foods.
This study had several limitations: first, it was based on a retrospective database, relying on
diagnoses performed by hundreds of physicians which may create some noise due, for
example, to differences in reporting. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study did not
allow for controlling for potential risk factors that are not present in the electronic health
records including diet. Second, the diagnosis of FA relied on very rigid criteria, and
consequently, the prevalence of FA was lower than expected. The allergic group was
significantly smaller than the nonallergic group. Therefore, including unconfirmed FA cases in
the allergic group would have a stronger influence on the results. Third, there is a time gap that
differs between cases in the time elapsed from the first suspected allergic reaction and until the
formal diagnosis is made, therefore relying on medical diagnosis cannot assess the impact of
age at first reaction. Fourth, due to absence of dietary data in the electronic health records, we
do not have information regarding the timing of allergenic food introduction or the specific FA.
The fact that we could not find solid variables that influence the risk of developing FA might
suggest that the introduction timing, as suggested in previous studies [6‒9, 33], can be a
significant factor influencing the development of FA.

Conclusions
Birthweight, SES, ethnicity, and AD are the main risk factors for the development of FA but do
not explain all cases. This study aimed to widen the search for risk factors to unobvious clinical
parameters that could explain other cases of FA. Although we find additional novel factors that
correlate with the development of FA, the clinical significance of those variables is questionable.
This may strengthen the underlying assumption that supports age and the root of exposure to
food allergens as major factors influencing the development of FA. Additional studies are
needed to establish either the influence of the correlations we found or the effect of the timing
of food allergen exposure on the development of FA.

Background
The age-standardized smoking rates were 25.6% among men and 6.9% among women in Japan in 2023 [
1
]. Smoking significantly increases the risk of severe health problems, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory illnesses, and cancer [
2
–
4
]. It is also closely related to mental disorders. The smoking rate and proportion of heavy smokers are higher among individuals with psychiatric diseases than in the general population [
5
]. Recent research has shown that, compared with never smokers, former smokers have 1.08-fold higher odds of depressive symptoms, whereas current smokers have 1.24-fold higher odds [
6
].
Although the physical and mental harms of smoking are well established, evidence shows that their detrimental effects are reversible following smoking cessation. For example, the excess risk of pulmonary disease due to smoking can be eliminated within 20 years of cessation [
7
]. Among light former smokers, the risk of cardiovascular disease significantly decreases within 10 years of cessation [
8
]. Similar trends have been observed for mental health. Data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey of adults across all age groups indicate that longer smoking abstinence is associated with fewer depressive symptoms [
9
].
Despite the well-established benefits of smoking cessation, nicotine addiction remains a major barrier [
10
]. Moreover, smoking cessation may be more challenging among older adults because of long-standing nicotine dependence, entrenched smoking habits, and underestimation of their susceptibility to smoking-related harm [
11
]. However, substantial health gains have also been demonstrated among those who quit later in life; for example, quitting at age 65 years is associated with a 23.4% higher probability of surviving for at least one additional year, and cessation at age 75 years still increases survival by approximately 14.2% [
12
].
Evidence also suggests that smoking cessation may be associated with better mental health outcomes. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that successful smoking cessation was associated with better mental health outcomes, and a large longitudinal cohort study similarly found that smoking cessation was associated with lower levels of common mental health symptoms [
13
,
14
]. However, these studies primarily included middle-aged or mixed-age populations. In contrast, a 1-year longitudinal study reported that smoking cessation after age 55 years was associated with a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms compared with continued smoking [
15
]. Thus, the association between smoking cessation and depressive symptoms in later life remains unclear, and few studies have specifically focused on community-dwelling older adults. Because late-life depressive symptoms are strongly associated with reduced quality of life and increased mortality, clarifying this association has important clinical and public health implications [
16
,
17
].
Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations between duration since smoking cessation, age at smoking cessation, and depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults.
Methods
Study design and participants
This study enrolled participants from the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology–Study of Geriatric Syndromes (NCGG-SGS), conducted across several sites in Aichi Prefecture, Japan [
18
]. The NCGG-SGS is a large community-based cohort study designed to identify the risks of geriatric syndromes associated with aging and to determine effective ways to manage them. We performed a cross-sectional analysis of data from 20,950 older adults who participated in the NCGG-SGS in four municipalities: Tokai, Toyoake, Chita, and Midori Ward of Nagoya. All individuals residing in the survey areas at the time of the study, including those aged ≥60 years in Chita, ≥65 years in Tokai and Toyoake, and ≥70 years in Midori Ward, Nagoya City, were eligible for inclusion. The survey was conducted from September 2017 to August 2024: Tokai (September 2017–June 2018), Toyoake (September 2017–June 2018), Chita (October 2019–March 2021), and Midori Ward, Nagoya City (June 2023–August 2024). Data were collected during face-to-face assessments. Nurses interviewed participants to obtain their medical histories and medication use. Other baseline characteristics, including smoking habits and depressive symptoms, were assessed by well-trained research personnel. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: certification for long-term care or support under Japan’s public long-term care insurance system (n = 152), disability in basic activities of daily living (n = 43), a history of dementia (n = 45), severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination score <21; n = 381), a history of Parkinson’s disease (n = 54), or missing information required to determine eligibility for these exclusion criteria (n = 88).
Variables
Participants were classified as never, former, or current smokers. For participants with a history of smoking, we recorded the age at smoking initiation and, for former smokers, the age at smoking cessation. Duration since smoking cessation was defined as the difference between the participant’s current age and age at smoking cessation. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), with scores ≥6 indicating depressive symptoms [
19
–
21
]. This cutoff has been validated for Japanese older adults [
22
]. Based on previous studies [
23
,
24
], covariates included age, sex, number of prescribed medications, presence of each comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or respiratory disease), equivalized income (<2 million JPY, 2–4 million JPY, or >4 million JPY) [
25
,
26
], living alone, exercise frequency (≥1 day per week or <1 day per week), sleep satisfaction (satisfied or dissatisfied), Mini-Mental State Examination score, and alcohol consumption. Additionally, the municipality (Tokai City, Toyoake City, Chita City, and Midori Ward of Nagoya City) was also included as a covariate to account for potential differences in participant recruitment and population characteristics across study areas.
Statistical analyses
Missing data were handled as follows. Smoking-related variables (smoking status, duration since smoking cessation, and age at smoking cessation), which were the primary exposure variables, and depressive symptoms (GDS-15 score), which was the outcome variable, were not imputed. Participants with missing data on these variables were excluded from the analyses (n = 8 for smoking-related variables and n = 37 for depressive symptoms). Among the remaining eligible participants, 1,592 had missing data for one or more covariates (number of missing values: prescribed medications, n = 26; hypertension, n = 1; diabetes, n = 9; stroke, n = 3; respiratory disease, n = 7; equivalized income, n = 1,531; number of household members, n = 241; exercise frequency, n = 9; sleep satisfaction, n = 3; alcohol consumption, n = 1). Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations. The imputation model included all variables used in the primary analyses. A total of 50 imputed datasets were generated and combined using Rubin’s rules.
Continuous variables were expressed as means (standard deviations [SDs]), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without depressive symptoms using the t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics across smoking status groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance or chi-square test.
To visualize the association between duration since smoking cessation and depressive symptoms, we modeled it using restricted cubic splines and presented the results as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with current smokers (0 years since smoking cessation) as the reference [
27
]. The spline models were adjusted for the covariates described in the Variables section using the multiply imputed datasets. Four knots were utilized, with the outer percentiles set at 0.05 and 0.95.
Moreover, based on a previous study [
28
], we examined whether the proportion of participants with depressive responses for each GDS-15 item changed with increasing duration since smoking cessation using the Cochran–Armitage trend test to identify the GDS-15 subdomains most closely associated with smoking cessation.
To identify factors associated with depressive symptoms, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses and estimated AORs with 95% CIs using the multiply imputed datasets. The dependent variable was depressive symptoms (GDS score ≥6). The exposure variable was smoking history. Participants were categorized into 10 groups: current smokers; those who had quit smoking for <4, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, 13–15, 16–18, 19–21, or ≥22 years; and never smokers. The models were adjusted for the covariates described in the Variables section.
Additionally, to examine whether smoking cessation initiated after entering older adulthood was associated with depressive symptoms, former smokers were categorized according to age at smoking cessation (<65 years or ≥65 years). Age 65 years was selected a priori because it is the conventional threshold for defining older adulthood in public health statistics and epidemiological studies, including national aging statistics in Japan and international indicators of the older population. In addition, smoking cessation after age 65 remains clinically meaningful, as previous studies have shown health benefits even when cessation occurs later in life [
12
]. We performed a separate multivariable logistic regression analysis in which participants were classified according to smoking history as current smokers, those who had quit smoking before age 65 years, those who had quit smoking at age ≥65 years, and never smokers. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30 (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan) or R version 4.5.0, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding smoking history, 6.6% were current smokers, 32.0% were former smokers, and 61.4% had never smoked. The prevalence of depressive symptoms (GDS score ≥6) was 11.9%. Association between duration since smoking cessation and depressive symptoms
We employed restricted cubic spline analysis to examine the nonlinear association between duration since smoking cessation and depressive symptoms. After adjusting for age and sex, a longer duration since smoking cessation was associated with a lower probability of depressive symptoms (Fig.

Using a multivariable-adjusted model, we examined the association between duration since smoking cessation and depressive symptoms. Discussion
We found that a longer duration since smoking cessation was associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults. After adjustment for potential confounders, individuals who had abstained from smoking for ≥7 years had significantly lower odds of depressive symptoms than current smokers. In addition, lower odds were observed among individuals who quit smoking after age 65 years. Despite having substantially shorter durations of abstinence, these individuals also had significantly lower odds of depressive symptoms than current smokers. This finding complements the primary duration-based analysis by suggesting that lower odds of depressive symptoms were observed even among participants who had quit smoking later in life.
Previous studies have reported that smoking is associated with depressive symptoms and other common mental health conditions [
6
,
13
,
14
]. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain this association. Nicotine promotes the release of serotonin and dopamine, producing transient pleasure; however, because nicotine is highly addictive and has a short half-life, repeated cycles of nicotine exposure and withdrawal may contribute to mood instability and depressive symptoms [
29
]. In addition, smoking has been associated with chronic systemic inflammation, which has been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression [
30
]. However, the mechanisms underlying the association between longer smoking abstinence and a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms remain less well understood. Previous studies have suggested that smoking cessation is associated with neurobiological changes related to nicotine withdrawal and adaptation, as well as gradual reductions in systemic inflammation over time [
31
,
32
]. In this context, the lower prevalence of depressive symptoms observed among long-term former smokers may be attributable to recovery from some of the adverse biological effects associated with chronic smoking exposure.
Moreover, long-term smoking abstinence may be accompanied by improvements in several factors associated with depressive symptoms in older adults. Taste perception has been shown to improve within weeks to months after smoking cessation [
33
,
34
]. Exercise tolerance, respiratory function, and respiratory symptoms such as cough and dyspnea also improve progressively over time [
35
–
37
]. Sleep quality also improves [
38
], and engagement in physical activity tends to increase [
39
]. These changes may indirectly contribute to better mental health among long-term former smokers. Nevertheless, the mechanisms linking long-term smoking abstinence and depressive symptoms remain uncertain and should be investigated in future longitudinal and mechanistic studies. In addition, in sensitivity analyses excluding sleep satisfaction, several associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. This may reflect the complex role of sleep as both a potential confounder and a possible mediator linking smoking cessation and depressive symptoms.
Previous studies examining the association between smoking cessation and cognitive decline have demonstrated the benefits of quitting smoking after approximately 20 years [
27
]. In contrast, our study found no such plateau. The prevalence of depressive symptoms continued to decrease with a longer duration since smoking cessation. One possible explanation for this pattern is the cumulative effect of multiple physiological and behavioral improvements associated with smoking cessation. Sustained smoking abstinence is accompanied by improvements in taste perception, respiratory function, exercise tolerance, respiratory symptoms, sleep quality, physical activity, and overall health status [
33
–
39
]. These improvements are closely associated with mental health and may indirectly contribute to lower odds of depressive symptoms. Thus, the absence of a plateau effect in our findings may reflect continuous positive feedback, representing a virtuous cycle of physical and psychological recovery following smoking cessation.
To further explore which aspects of depressive symptoms were associated with smoking cessation, we conducted a subitem analysis of the GDS-15. Depressive symptoms encompass multiple dimensions, with characteristic affective features including decreased positive affect and increased negative affect. In the context of short-term smoking cessation within 6 months, reduced positive affect, manifesting as anhedonia, may be more strongly associated with smoking cessation success than depressed mood [
40
]. In the present study, subitem analysis of the GDS-15 revealed that a longer duration since smoking cessation was associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms in the Dysphoric Mood, Withdrawal–Apathy–Vigor, and Hopelessness domains. These results suggest that long-term smoking cessation may be particularly associated with improvements in specific affective dimensions of depressive symptoms among older adults.
This study has some limitations. First, because of its cross-sectional design, a causal association between smoking cessation and depressive symptoms could not be determined. Some individuals smoke to alleviate depressive symptoms by stimulating these neurotransmitters, a concept known as the self-medication hypothesis. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether a longer duration since smoking cessation is associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms. In addition, sleep satisfaction and other covariates included in the analyses may have complex temporal relationships with both smoking cessation and depressive symptoms. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships among smoking cessation, depression-related factors, and depressive symptoms. Second, information on smoking behavior, including age at smoking cessation, was obtained through self-report. Therefore, recall bias cannot be ruled out, and actual smoking histories may differ from the reported data. Third, this study was conducted in a single prefecture of Japan. Thus, the generalizability of these findings to other regions or countries may be limited.
Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It included a large sample of community-dwelling older adults, which enhanced statistical power and external validity. Data were collected by trained nurses and research staff, ensuring high reliability except for variables potentially affected by recall bias. Moreover, the analyses accounted for key factors closely related to depressive symptoms, such as income and sleep satisfaction, allowing for a more accurate assessment of the association between duration since smoking cessation and depressive symptoms. These strengths support the robustness of our findings and provide valuable insights into smoking cessation as a potentially modifiable factor that may be associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults.
These findings have important clinical and public health implications. Although earlier smoking cessation remains desirable, our findings suggest that smoking cessation may remain associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms even when initiated after age 65 years. At the same time, the lower prevalence of depressive symptoms observed among individuals with longer durations of abstinence underscores the importance of maintaining long-term smoking cessation. Together, these findings support both early smoking cessation and sustained cessation support throughout middle and older adulthood.
Conclusions
Among community-dwelling older adults, a longer duration since smoking cessation was associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms. Even after adjustment for multiple covariates, participants who had quit smoking for ≥7 years had a significantly lower prevalence of depressive symptoms than current smokers. Furthermore, those who quit smoking after age 65 years had significantly lower odds of depressive symptoms than current smokers. These findings suggest the importance of both early smoking cessation and long-term support to maintain abstinence. Although smoking cessation should ideally occur as early as possible, our findings also suggest that smoking cessation may remain associated with better mental health even when initiated later in life. Together, these findings support continued efforts to promote smoking cessation and sustained abstinence among middle-aged and older adults.

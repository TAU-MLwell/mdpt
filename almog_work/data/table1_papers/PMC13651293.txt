Introduction
Circulating endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), initially identified in human peripheral blood in 1997 as cluster of differentiation (CD)34
+
mononuclear cells, were stem cells mainly derived from bone marrow [
1
]. EPCs acted as a critical reservoir for vascular repair and angiogenesis [
2
]. Current knowledge defined circulating EPCs as blood cells expressing both progenitor markers (e.g., CD34 and CD133) and endothelial markers (e.g., vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2, also named kinase insert domain receptor (KDR)) [
3
]. The number and function of EPCs declined along the aging process [
4
].
Aging is characterized by a progressive, systemic decline in physiological function, marked by reduced cellular turnover, accumulation of molecular damage, and diminished reserve capacity [
5
]. Population aging was a major driver of the cardiovascular disease (CVD) epidemic. With the rapid expansion of the aging population, the burden of CVD was expected to increase substantially and exacerbated in individuals with cardiovascular risk factors [
6
]. Advancing aging was associated with vascular endothelial dysfunction, as evidenced by reduced arterial endothelium-dependent dilation (EDD) in response to chemical or mechanical stimuli. Decreased nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, largely due to oxidative stress, was a key mechanism underlying aging-related impairment of EDD [
7
]. In general, aging induced senescence in endothelial cell lineages, including EPCs, leading to impaired vascular repair capacity and contributing to the development of CVD [
8
]. With advancing age, these cells exhibited reduced viability, diminished proliferative capacity, impaired differentiation potential, and increased senescence [
9
]. In the literature, reduced amounts and increased senescence of EPCs correlated with high Framingham Risk Score (FRS), endothelial dysfunction, and accelerated vascular aging, making them independent predictors of adverse cardiovascular events [
10
,
11
]. Detection of cellular senescence was therefore important for identifying and mitigating cardiovascular risk, as the accumulation of senescent and dysfunctional EPCs drove vascular aging and atherosclerosis. Such detection may enable targeted interventions, including senolytic therapies that removed senescent cells, reduced inflammation, and enhanced vascular repair [
8
]. Methods for detecting senescent EPCs typically involved flow cytometry for surface markers, senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assays, and analysis of DNA damage markers [
8
,
12
]. We recently reported that Pannexin 1 (Panx1) expression was upregulated in human circulating CD34
+
mononuclear cells and the derived endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) respectively during aging and replication-induced senescence [
13
]. Senescence-associated indicators, including SA-β-gal, p16
INK4a
, p21, acetyl-p53, and phospho-Histone H2A.X increased by replication-induced senescence in ECFCs could be reduced by Panx1 down-regulation. We also found that angiogenic potential of replication-induced senescent ECFCs was improved by Panx1 reduction through increased IGF-1 production via activation of FAK-ERK axis. Conversely, angiogenic activities of young ECFCs was impaired by Panx1 overexpression. In addition, overexpression of Panx1 in ECFCs reduced total endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). These findings provided new strategies to evaluate EPC activities in CVD patients [
13
].
Panx1 was an ATP-permeable channel that was ubiquitously expressed and formed heptameric channels in the plasma membrane, mediating paracrine and autocrine signaling [
14
]. Panx1 played key roles in various physiological processes, including blood pressure regulation, apoptotic cell clearance, and human oocyte development [
15
]. Panx1 channels were canonically implicated in ATP release, particularly during inflammation, and their role in inflammasome activation and interleukin (IL)-1β secretion by macrophages were well documented [
16
]. However, whether Panx1 may serve as a reliable biomarker of senescence/aging remained unclear.
In the present study, we hypothesized that Panx1 could serve as a senescence marker associated with cardiovascular risk and aimed to investigate the role of Panx1 expression in human circulating progenitor marker-positive cells during senescence/aging and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of Panx1-associated senescence/aging in a real-world, non-diabetic population.

Evaluation of the expression of Panx1, SA-β-Gal, and NO in circulating progenitor marker-expressing cells using multicolor flow cytometry
For detection of senescent status and Panx1 expression in circulating progenitor-marker positive cells, PBMCs were blocking with 10% FBS in MACS buffer (pH 7.2 of PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin and 2 mM EDTA) at room temperature for 10 min. After MACS buffer wash, PBMCs were resuspended in 500 µL of MACS buffer and then incubated with SPiDER-β gal (1:1000, Dojindo, Ex/ Em: 493/ 560 nm) at 37 ℃ for 15 min. After another MACS buffer wash, cells were resuspended in 200 µL of MACS buffer and then labeled with anti-Panx1 conjugated allophycocyanin (APC, 1:100, Santa Cruz, clone D-12), anti-CD133 conjugated Phycoerythrin (PE, 1:100, Miltenyi Biotec), anti-CD34 conjugated electron coupled dye (ECD, 1:20, BECKMAN COULTER), anti-KDR conjugated Phycoerythrin-Cyanine 7 (PC7, 1:20, BECKMAN COULTER), and anti-CD45 conjugated Krome Orange (KrO, 1:40, BECKMAN COULTER) under 4℃ for 30 min, simultaneously. In addition, the isotype antibodies were also conducted according to above condition. Thereafter, cells were washed twice and resuspended in 500 µL of MACS buffer before analysis under flow cytometer.
For detection of nitric oxide and Panx1 expression in circulating progenitor cell marker positive cells, PBMCs were incubated with 1 µM of nitric oxide staining dye (Abcam, Ex/ Em: 540/590 nm) modified from diaminofluorescein-FM diacetate (DAF-FM DA) at 37 ℃ for 30 min and then treated with 1 mM of NONOate (diazeniumdiolates, NO donor) at 37 ℃ for another 30 min as positive control. Unstained PBMCs were served as negative control. After MACS buffer wash, cells were labeled with anti-Panx1 conjugated FITC (1:100, Santa Cruz, clone D-12), anti-CD34 conjugated ECD (1:20, BECKMAN COULTER), anti-CD133 conjugated APC (1:100, Miltenyi Biotec), anti-KDR conjugated PC7 (1:20, BECKMAN COULTER), and anti-CD45 conjugated KrO (1:40, BECKMAN COULTER) under 4℃ for 30 min, simultaneously. In addition, the isotype antibodies were also conducted according to above condition. Thereafter, cells were washed twice and resuspended in 500 µL of MACS buffer before analysis under flow cytometer.
For flow cytometry, a total of 50,000 events were collected under CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer possessing 16 detectors and 4 Lasers (model: N0-V5-B3-Y5-R3-I0, BECKMAN COULTER, USA). Compensation matrix were obtained from analysis of the gain value of unstained, SPiDER-β gal staining, NO staining dye, and antibody single staining cells under instrument settings. Analysis of TNF-α and IL-6 concentration in human plasma
For TNF-α and IL-6 measurement, 5 ml peripheral blood was collected from donors using BD Vacutainer™ EDTA blood collection tube. After centrifuge at 1000 g for 15 min, the plasma was collected and stored at -80 ℃ before experiments. TNF-α and IL-6 were detected by ELISA kits (R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The absorbance was read at 450 nm and corrected with 540 nm.
Framingham Risk Score (FRS)
FRS was separately calculated in elderly participants of the two cohorts using the published 2008 general cardiovascular disease calculator (
https://reference.medscape.com/calculator/252/framingham-risk-score-2008
). Its inputs are sex, age, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, SBP, treatment for hypertension, smoking, diabetes, and known vascular disease. Moderate-to-high cardiovascular risk was defined as FRS ≥ 10%.
Statistical analysis
Continuous data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as appropriate. Baseline characteristics in Tables
1
and
2
are presented as mean ± SEM, whereas group comparisons displayed in the figures are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (Supplementary Table
S3
, Panel A). Spearman rank correlation was the primary correlation method when either variable was non-normal; Pearson correlation was used as a sensitivity analysis when distributional assumptions were compatible. Partial correlations adjusted sequentially for age or age group and for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and fasting plasma glucose (FPG). Because recruitment comprised two widely separated age strata, age-group adjustment and within-stratum analyses were emphasized for pooled correlations. Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction was applied within prespecified analysis families and across the exploratory Framingham Risk Score (FRS) correlation matrix. Multivariable logistic regression for FRS ≥ 10% reported odds ratios per 1-standard-deviation increase in Panx1. Discrimination was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) with 2,000-iteration bootstrap confidence intervals and Harrell optimism correction. Incremental value was assessed by change in AUC (ΔAUC), continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Models with low events per variable (EPV) were treated as sensitivity analyses. Effect sizes for continuous between-group comparisons were expressed as Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals; categorical variables used Cramér’s V and Cohen’s h. For the systolic blood pressure (SBP) comparison in Table
2
, post hoc power was estimated with a two-sided independent-samples t test at α = 0.05 (Supplementary Table
S4
, Panel A). Analyses were performed on all participants with non-missing data for the variables concerned (available-case analysis); no imputation was undertaken. Accordingly, the denominator was 50 for analyses restricted to Panx1, SA-β-gal and NO-related fluorescence, and 49 for any analysis incorporating TNF-α or IL-6, including the corresponding correlation, partial-correlation and multivariable models. All tests were two-sided, with
p
< 0.05 considered nominally significant.
Between-group analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5, and correlation, regression and biomarker-performance analyses were conducted using Python 3.10.5. Normally distributed two-group comparisons used unpaired t tests, and comparisons of more than two groups used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey post-hoc testing; non-normal data used Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests with the two-stage Benjamini-Krieger-Yekutieli procedure, as appropriate. All reported correlation analyses followed the normality-guided and adjusted framework described above.
Because FRS is a deterministic function of the inputs, they were not entered as covariates in models with FRS as the outcome; adjustment focused on variables not used to construct the score, including BMI, fasting plasma glucose, eGFR, statin use, antihypertensive drug classes, TNF-α and IL-6, as available. The number of antihypertensive drug classes was derived as the count of distinct pharmacological classes prescribed per participant (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, angiotensin II receptor blocker, calcium-channel blocker, β-blocker, α-blocker and diuretic, each scored 0 or 1), so that multiple agents within the same class contributed only once; statin use was coded as a binary variable.

Discussion
The present study addresses three distinct questions in a real-world, non-diabetic population: (1) whether Panx1 tracks cellular senescence independently of chronological age; (2) whether Panx1 is independently associated with NO-related or inflammatory signals; and (3) whether Panx1 provides information on cardiovascular risk beyond conventional clinical factors. The findings were based on two independent discovery and phenotyping cohorts of non-diabetic participants respectively for mRNA and flow cytometry examination. The phenotyping cohort was not for validation of the discovery cohort. Because the study is cross-sectional, these analyses assess associations and discrimination rather than prediction or causality.
Panx1 functioned as a gateway for ATP release, linking signals from damaged cells to activation of the innate immune system. Its activity often established a positive feedback loop that amplified inflammation, making it a potential therapeutic target for inflammatory disorders [
19
]. In addition, flow cytometry, a powerful high-throughput technology used to analyze a wide range of physical and chemical characteristics of individual cells [
20
], was performed in the present study to further evaluate the PBMCs defined by CD surface markers. CD markers were surface proteins used to classify and isolate specific cell types, primarily within the immune system [
21
]. CD45, a tyrosine phosphatase expressed on all nucleated leukocytes across both lymphoid and myeloid lineages [
22
], was a pan-leukocyte marker expressed on nearly all white blood cells. By defining EPCs as CD45⁻, researchers distinguished these progenitor cells from hematopoietic cells, focusing on those with endothelial potential [
23
,
24
]. While CD45⁻ served as a baseline for defining progenitor cells associated with angiogenesis and cardiovascular health, these cells were typically co-identified by other surface markers, most commonly CD34⁺, CD133⁺, and KDR⁺ [
3
,
25
]. To date, there has been no universally accepted “CD classification”; identification has relied on combinations of hematopoietic markers (CD34, CD133) and endothelial markers (KDR). Circulating EPCs derived from PBMCs were typically classified by CD surface markers, including CD34, CD133, and KDR. Common EPC phenotypes include CD34⁺KDR⁺ (more differentiated and canonical) and CD34⁺CD133⁺KDR⁺ (more primitive) [
24
,
26
].
In the literature, few studies investigated the relationship between aging and circulating CD34⁺ and/or CD133⁺ cells. Higher levels of CD34
+
circulating progenitor cells had been shown to predict longer survival in the oldest old, highlighting their potential prognostic value in individuals aged 80 years and above [
27
]. Additionally, one study reported a differential increase in CD34
+
, KDR
+
CD34
+
, CD133
+
CD34
+
, and CD117
+
CD34
+
populations on days 3 and 4 after acute myocardial infarction [
28
]. Investigating cellular senescence was fundamental to aging research. Spider β-gal, a highly sensitive and cell-permeable fluorescent probe, enabled detection of SA-β-gal, a principal biomarker of senescent cells [
29
]. Apart from our previous study showing association of Panx1 with cellular senescence and aging [
13
], a recent study reported that Panx1, via its noncanonical function, contributed to cellular senescence and renal fibrosis after acute kidney injury [
30
]. However, the role of Panx1 in cellular senescence and aging, relative to SA-β-gal, has not been previously investigated.
Aging, together with cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, is well known to promote microvascular dysfunction, arterial inflammation, and plaque formation, leading to end organ damage [
31
–
33
]. In the context of aging, inflammaging is a well-recognized phenomenon in which many older individuals develop a persistent, low-grade systemic pro-inflammatory state [
34
,
35
]. This condition is characterized by elevated circulating levels of pro-inflammatory markers, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and C-reactive protein, which are considered indicators of biological aging. It is also a major risk factor for, and contributor to, numerous age-related diseases, including ASCVD, type 2 diabetes, dementia, and frailty [
35
,
36
]. Regarding endothelial function, NO was a protective, anti-inflammatory molecule and its reduced bioavailability was a key driving force on vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [
37
,
38
]. NO has been shown to prevent hypoxia-induced mitochondrial oxidative damage and to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis in EPCs [
39
,
40
]. In addition, our previous finding that, in circulating CD34
+
PBMCs, higher Panx1 protein expression was higher in aged people, compared to young people was based on a small number of people [
13
]. Since Western blotting of circulating CD34
+
PBMCs used in that study was time-consuming and expensive when applied to a larger number of participants, in the first part of the present study, we tested whether Panx1 mRNA analysis of PBMCs, more applicable (less time-consuming and cheaper, compared to Western blotting) in the real world, could be used to detect senescence/aging and even related to cardiovascular risk in the aged donors, and the findings were positive. Thereafter, we explored the expression of Panx1 in various populations of progenitor marker-expressing cells and the clinical implications. In this study, NO activity assessed in non-diabetic individuals reflects intracellular NO-related fluorescence measured at the individual cell level rather than systemic endothelial NO bioavailability.
The most robust finding regarding senescence of the present study was compartment-specific. Associations between Panx1 and SA-β-gal persisted after age and clinical adjustment in CD34
+
KDR
+
CD45
−
and CD133
+
KDR
+
CD45
−
populations, whereas non-KDR associations were attenuated by age. This pattern argues against treating Panx1 as a universal substitute for SA-β-gal. Rather, Panx1 may capture a senescence-related phenotype in selected endothelial-lineage progenitor populations. The significant age-group interaction is hypothesis-generating and requires replication.
The adjusted analyses did not support an independent link between Panx1 and reduced NO-related fluorescence or circulating TNF-α and IL-6. Although the present study showed that, in cell culture experiments of ECFCs, Panx1 and NO influence each other, in the real-world assessment of PBMCs, the primary and adjusted analyses for NO-related fluorescence were null across all four populations; the isolated elderly CD133
+
CD45
−

This study has several limitations. The cohorts were small, single-center samples of non-diabetic Taiwanese volunteers, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The cross-sectional design, together with the lack of external validation and functional testing, precludes conclusions regarding causality or predictive performance. Recruitment into two widely separated age strata may inflate pooled correlations; therefore, age-group-adjusted and within-stratum analyses were performed, although residual confounding by age cannot be completely excluded. The sample size precluded comprehensive covariate adjustment, and medication use was collinear with its indications; thus, analyses with low EPV and multiplicity-adjusted results should be considered exploratory. The systolic blood pressure comparison achieved 78.2% power at the observed effect size (d = 0.79), marginally below the conventional 80% threshold (Supplementary Table
S4
, Panel A), and no formal power analysis was performed for the multivariable models. Independent prospective validation in larger cohorts is warranted to confirm these findings.
Conclusions
Panx1 expression increases with aging and is associated with SA-β-gal expression in selected circulating CD34⁺KDR⁺CD45⁻ progenitor populations after adjustment for age and relevant clinical covariates. Among elderly participants, the number of Panx1⁺ cells within the CD34⁺CD45⁻ population is associated with Framingham cardiovascular risk and show moderate discrimination for FRS ≥ 10%; however, whether it provides incremental value beyond conventional clinical factors remains uncertain. Adjusted analyses do not support an independent association with NO-related fluorescence or systemic inflammatory markers. Panx1 may therefore represent a candidate cellular senescence-associated marker associated with cardiovascular risk, warranting further prospective validation.
